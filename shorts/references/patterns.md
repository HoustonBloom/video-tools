# Recurring shapes, and the clips each one owes

A talk recording is not a flat field of quotable lines. It has a small number of
shapes that recur across episodes, and each shape owes a known set of clips. This
file is the catalogue: what to look for, how to spot it, and what comes out.

**The point is coverage rather than taste.** Reading a transcript looking for good
lines produces whatever the reader happened to notice. Walking the catalogue
produces the clips the episode owes, and makes a miss visible.

Written from one episode of a founder talk show. Every pattern below is one
that episode contained, with the count it produced.

---

## How to run it on a new episode

1. **Segment the recording first**, from the run of show and the tape. A segment
   is a stretch with one shape in it.
2. **Walk the catalogue against each segment.** Most segments match one pattern.
   Some match none, which is a fact rather than a failure.
3. **Count what each match owes** and write it down before writing any proposal.
4. **Compare the count to what got proposed.** A segment that owes clips and
   produced none is the finding.

Step 4 is the one that matters. On that episode the first proposal offered
thirteen clips and none of them came from the couch introductions, which is the
one segment where the show has promised a clip in writing. The gap was invisible
in a list and obvious the moment segments and clips were put on the same axis.

**So: put the clips on the timeline.** A proposal surface that shows, per
segment, where the proposed clips fall inside it, exposes an empty segment at a
glance. That view is the check, not decoration.

## The patterns

### 1. One question, many answers

**Looks like:** a question addressed to the room rather than to a person, then
several people take the microphone in turn.

**Owes:** one clip per responder, each opening on the same question, plus one
montage of all the answers. Five responders is six clips.

Full detail in `series.md`, including the montage the beat contract refuses.
That episode: one instance, six clips.

### 2. The featured segment

**Looks like:** a named guest with a scheduled slot.

**Owes:** at least one clip per guest, and the guest block on the proposal needs
four things: what the thing is in their own words, the vision, the interesting
part, and why they built it.

**Propose these first.** They are the reason the episode exists, and the guest is
the person most likely to post what they are sent. That episode: two featured
founders, two clips, and both were missing from the first proposal.

### 3. The self-introduction round

**Looks like:** each person in turn giving their name and what they are building,
usually a minute each, usually with a time limit stated by the host.

**Owes:** one clip per person, the span being their whole turn. No hook, build
and payoff contract applies: the form is `intro` and the clip is the turn.

Two properties make this the cheapest quality clip in any episode:

- **It is self-attributing.** The person says their own name and their own
  company inside the clip, so it can carry a name where an unattributed quote
  elsewhere cannot. Still check spelling against the registration list, because
  transcription mangles surnames.
- **It is often contractually owed.** On one show the run of show says
  "Each person gets their own clip after the show", and a couch seat is sold with
  that included.

That episode: four introductions, four clips, **zero proposed on the first pass.**

### 4. The disagreement

**Looks like:** a question put to the group, then two or more people taking
incompatible positions, and nobody conceding. Distinct from a discussion, where
positions converge.

**Owes:** one multi-speaker clip, one span per position, ordered so the strongest
claim lands last.

This is the one shape where splicing is free in camera-only footage, because
every join is a speaker change and reads as coverage rather than as a jump cut.
That episode: one instance, four positions, one clip.

### 5. The origin

**Looks like:** past-tense frustration followed by a decision verb. "I got tired
of X, so I built it." Almost every founder gives one, usually early, usually in
answer to how they knew the problem was real.

**Owes:** a clip, or the opening beat of the founder's main clip.

### 6. The inversion

**Looks like:** a comparison to a familiar system that reframes the problem.
That episode's was hiring against Uber: before Uber you stood in the road waving
at traffic, and a job description is the same gesture.

**Owes:** hook material. An inversion is the strongest opening a talk produces,
because it makes the familiar thing look absurd before any argument arrives.

### 7. The number that should not be true

**Looks like:** a date, a count or a ratio that sounds wrong. "Resumes were
invented in the 1400s." "How many creators can one operator run."

**Owes:** hook material, **and a verification task.** The number is the whole
clip, so if it is wrong the clip is wrong. Write the check into the proposal as
an open item rather than assuming.

### 8. The refusal

**Looks like:** somebody declining the obviously rational move, on the record.
"You can throw millions at me, but at the same time my morals are there."

**Owes:** a hook. A refusal opens an information gap by itself: the viewer needs
to know why.

### 9. The admission

**Looks like:** a claim immediately undercut by the speaker. "It's very difficult
to do, but that's what I'm trying to do."

**Owes:** keeping. This is the pattern most often cut out by mistake, because it
reads as weak on the page and reads as honest on camera. Cutting it makes the
speaker sound more certain than they were, which is a small misrepresentation.

### 10. The closing story

**Looks like:** a personal anecdote in the last five minutes, often involving
family, told after the host signals the wrap.

**Owes:** a clip, usually the most human one in the episode. That episode's was a
parent asked by an eleven year old what he would do with a billion dollars.

Watch the segment boundary here. The closing story sits after the last agenda
item and gets swallowed by whatever segment precedes it if the boundaries are
drawn from the run of show alone.

## Anti-patterns: shapes that look cuttable and are not

### The arithmetic that does not close

**Looks like:** a method walked through with numbers, usually an exit or a
funnel. Check every multiplier stated against every figure stated.

That episode's example had a stated sale price, a stated multiple and a stated
monthly revenue that could not all be true together. The speaker said three times
that the numbers were invented, which protects him in a room and does not protect
him in a forty second clip where the numbers are the entire content.

**Owes:** a refusal section in the proposal, plus the one line from it that
survives without the numbers.

### The borrowed line

**Looks like:** a memorable line the speaker attributes, or half-attributes, to
something they read. Detect on "I read", "there was a writing", "they say",
"someone said".

**Owes:** nothing. Cutting it as the speaker's own is a misattribution, and the
best lines in a talk are disproportionately borrowed ones.

### The passage that needs a person's permission

Every episode has some. Employment status, allegations about third parties,
named or identifiable people, personal security details, politics.

**Owes:** a hold, per passage, with a severity and a note on whether it sits
inside a proposed clip. Two of That episode's eight sat inside proposed clips.

## What this catalogue does not do

**It does not rank.** Walking it produces coverage, not a running order. Which
clip leads is a judgement, and it stays the human's.

**It is not measured on posted performance.** Every claim here about what makes a
clip worth cutting is structural. The instrument that would settle any of it is
the retention and share data on posted clips, which does not exist yet. When it
does, this file is where the disagreements get recorded.

**It is not complete.** It was written from one episode. Add a pattern when a
second episode shows it, and record the count it produced, so the catalogue keeps
earning its place rather than becoming a checklist nobody reads.

## Yield per segment, measured on that episode

The first thing walking the catalogue produced was not a clip. It was a shape in
the show itself.

| Segment | Ran | Clips | Per minute |
|---|---|---|---|
| Couch introductions | 2:41 | 4 | **1.49** |
| Audience questions | 6:49 | 5 | **0.73** |
| Closing remarks | 2:20 | 1 | 0.43 |
| Featured founder, second | 16:33 | 2 | 0.12 |
| Group conversation | 20:09 | 3 | 0.15 |
| Featured founder, first | 14:24 | 1 | 0.07 |

**The two long interviews take 31 of the 72 minutes and produce 3 of the 17
clips. The two short segments take 9 minutes and produce 9.**

Two things follow, and only the first is about editing.

**For cutting:** budget attention by yield, not by runtime. An interview is worth
one strong clip and rarely more, because it is one person developing one argument
at length. A round of introductions is worth one clip per person by construction.
An open floor is worth one per responder.

**For the show:** the densest segment on the sheet was the one with a time limit
and a fixed prompt, and the second densest was not on the run of show at all. If
an episode wants more clips, the lever is more short structured turns, not longer
interviews. That is a format observation from one episode and it is worth
watching across three before anyone acts on it.

**Not measured:** whether the dense segments produce clips anybody watches. Yield
is not performance. The instrument is the retention data on posted clips.
